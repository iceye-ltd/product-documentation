# 10\) Data Products

!!! summary inline end

    - SAR data products are organized into levels that reflect how far processing has progressed, from level 0 signal-domain products to level 2 analysis-ready imagery and derivatives.

    - Compensated Phase History Data (CPHD) is a standardized level 0 product that stores corrected phase history arrays and rich metadata for advanced custom processing and algorithm development.

    - Single Look Complex (SLC) images are foundational level 1 products that preserve full complex information (amplitude and phase) in slant-range geometry, enabling techniques such as InSAR and coherent change detection.

    - Ground Range Detected (GRD) images are amplitude-only, ground-projected products optimized for visualization and many GIS workflows, trading phase information for ease of use and file-size efficiency.

    - Sensor Independent Complex Data (SICD) and Sensor Independent Derived Data (SIDD) standardize complex and derived imagery across sensors and vendors using NITF-based containers, improving interoperability for advanced users.

    - Higher level 2 products such as Colorized Sub-Aperture Stacks (CSI) and SAR Video (VID) exploit sub-aperture and temporal information to support dynamic scene interpretation, motion analysis, and persistent monitoring.

Figure [<span class="underline">9.1</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#figure-9.1-sar-data-processing-flow-and-common-products) in the preceding chapter presented an overview of the processing flow and a simple view of the various data product types that can be generated from SAR data. In this chapter, we describe the introduced level 0 and 1 as well as two notable level 2 data products in more detail. Please refer to the ICEYE Data Product Specification to understand what data products ICEYE offers, their specifications, and finally how to order them. Unlike the specification, this chapter focuses on providing a general overview of the products known across the industry.

## 10.1) Compensated Phase History Data (CPHD)

<span class="underline">Compensated Phase History Data (CPHD)</span> provides detailed, corrected phase history information for advanced processing. As described in Chapter [<span class="underline">9</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md), it is a standardized level 0 data product that bridges the gap between raw phase history data and fully processed imagery. Moreover, it provides a sensor-independent data structure that can accommodate phase history signal arrays from diverse SAR platforms, including both monostatic and bistatic imaging systems.

### 10.1.1) Origins and Purpose

The CPHD emerged in the early 2000s from the need for a standardized format to handle phase history data from diverse SAR platforms while ensuring compatibility across various advanced SAR exploitation systems and workflows. It was designed through collaboration among government, defense, and research organizations to support advanced applications like target detection, change detection and terrain analysis. Its versatility, interoperability and high fidelity have guaranteed it to remain a cornerstone of modern SAR exploitation especially for researchers as well as in defense applications and specialized geospatial analysis.

### 10.1.2) Processing and Preparation

CPHD is generated by applying a series of corrections and calibrations to raw phase history data. These include compensation for hardware timing errors, platform motion, and Doppler effects to maintain phase coherence. This motion compensation is vital for advanced applications like interferometry, polarimetry, and target motion analysis and ensures accurate geolocation and compatibility with advanced SAR processing tools, while retaining the fidelity of the original radar returns.

### 10.1.3) Formats and Packaging

The CPHD format packages the phase history data as structured data arrays into a purpose-built binary file format that includes a structured header as well as an extensive metadata block that describes acquisition geometry, motion compensation, and sensor and calibration parameters. Commonly used metadata formats include XML to ensure compatibility and usability with various processing tools. This structure ensures that users can seamlessly integrate CPHD data into advanced SAR workflows, leveraging the detailed information provided for custom analysis and exploitation.

### 10.1.4) Applications and Use Cases

CPHD is primarily used in research, defense, and advanced geospatial analysis workflows that require access to the full radar phase history rather than to pre-focused image products. Because it preserves raw temporal and Doppler information, CPHD enables custom image formation, experimental processing chains, and specialized analyses that cannot be performed on standard SLC or SICD data.

Typical applications include the signal-domain techniques described in Section <span class="underline">9.9</span>, such as sub-aperture processing, micro-Doppler and vibration analysis, dynamic target motion extraction, and the derivation of advanced level 2 products like Colorized Sub-Aperture Stacks and SAR Video, described in Sections [<span class="underline">10.5</span>](#105-colorized-sub-aperture-stack-images-csi) and [<span class="underline">10.6</span>](#sar-video-vid). CPHD is also valuable for algorithm development, sensor characterization, and exploratory research where full control over focusing and compensation steps is required.

As a level 0 product, CPHD can be further processed into standard SAR imagery or tailored products, but doing so requires specialized SAR processing software and technical expertise. Users typically rely on custom implementations, research toolkits, or advanced signal-processing environments to unlock its full potential.

### 10.1.5) Advantages and Limitations

CPHD offers high fidelity and phase coherence, enabling advanced exploitation workflows. Its standardized sensor-independent format enhances interoperability, but the product demands significant expertise and specialized tools for effective use, as the phase history arrays cannot be directly processed and interpreted by common GIS tools. Additionally, the large data size can pose storage and processing challenges.

## 10.2) Single Look Complex (SLC) Images

As presented in Chapter [<span class="underline">9</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md), the <span class="underline">Single Look Complex (SLC) image</span> is the foundational level 1 SAR data product as it provides the full complex SAR backscatter data focused into an image in the technically natural slant range geometry. SLCs are integral for applications requiring precise phase information, such as Interferometric SAR (InSAR) and Coherent Change Detection (CCD), and serve as the basis for producing more refined data products.

### 10.2.1) Origins and Purpose

The development of SLC products arose with the 1978 launch of Seasat by NASA and the need to create an imaging data product that preserved the full SAR signal information for advanced applications. By retaining both amplitude and phase, SLCs enable several analytical techniques that are not possible with amplitude data alone.

The SLC product was designed to be the foundational SAR image data product from which all application-specific products could be derived, while already being an image itself, unlike the CPHD.

The processing steps and formats for SLC production were harmonized and standardized in the 1990s when SLCs were produced and leveraged internationally by spaceborne missions launched by the European Space Agency (ERS-1, ERS-2), Canada (RADASAT-1) and Japan (JERS-1).

The role of the SLC data product has and continues to be critical for a wide range of geospatial analytics, particularly those where coherence and geometric accuracy are paramount. These characteristics continue to make SLC products indispensable for scientific, commercial, and defense purposes.

### 10.2.2) Processing and Preparation

The production of SLC images goes beyond the processing steps required for the CPHD data products, as explained in Chapter [<span class="underline">9</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md): The PHD arrays are compressed in range and azimuth to focus the raw radar returns into a coherent image in the slant range coordinate system that corresponds to the sensor’s line of sight, that is, the satellite’s image acquisition geometry. Radiometric calibration is performed to ensure radar backscatter values are comparable across acquisitions. The image coordinate system is centered on the zero-Doppler (time of closest approach). SAR coordinates and data is arranged in the slant-range-by-azimuth imaging plane with data samples or pixels spaced equidistant in azimuth according to the inverse of the pulse repetition frequency, and in slant range according to the range sampling frequency. More refined motion and calibration compensation is also applied to map the data across the focused image coherently and to ensure high geometric accuracy.

As the name suggests, SLC images have only a single look and no multi-looking is performed. Therefore however, in most cases the impulse response function (the shape of a single, isolated radar-bright object in the radar image) is asymmetrical with azimuth resolution being smaller (finer) than range resolution. The benefit though is that the result offers the highest fidelity as it retains the full resolution and the complete phase information.

### 10.2.3) Formats and Packaging

SLC data is packaged with specialized SAR data container formats that support the efficient storage and accessibility of the full complex SAR data with no or very limited information loss (due to compression). The standard container file format for SLC data products has long been HDF5 but the GeoTIFF has increasingly taken its place in many user communities.

In a HDF5 file (such as the legacy SLC format defined in the ICEYE product spec), images are stored as binary matrices with each image pixel containing both amplitude and phase information but in the in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) domain, often in either the signed 16 bit integer or IEEE-754 single precision 32-bit floating point format. The structure of the binary data is shown in Figure [<span class="underline">10.1</span>](#figure-10.1-the-representation-of-slc-images-in-hdf5-and-geotiff-files). Each row of the matrix is a single range line (or profile) of the image with increasing range preceding from lower indices to higher indices (left to right in Figure [<span class="underline">10.1</span>](#figure-10.1-the-representation-of-slc-images-in-hdf5-and-geotiff-files)). Early row indices in the matrix correspond to early pulses and azimuth positions and later rows correspond to later pulses and azimuth positions (top to bottom in Figure [<span class="underline">10.1</span>](#figure-10.1-the-representation-of-slc-images-in-hdf5-and-geotiff-files)). The matrix in the figure represents the shadows-right azimuth-down arrangement that was presented already in Section [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#92-sample-arrangement).

Some algorithms process SLC imagery with increasing range aligned to increasing row index - often called shadows down orientation. This approach is usually used for fine resolution SAR imaging systems where it is important for target recognition to have a consistent shadow and layover alignment. As described in Section [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#92-sample-arrangement), it is often better for analysts to view SAR amplitude imagery shadows down as opposed to North-up because the human visual system prefers that orientation to properly interpret topography and elevated features. However, any image storage scheme can be manipulated on an image workstation to present images shadows down.

HDF5 accommodates the complete set of metadata within the file’s header structure in the XML format. The metadata that comes with ICEYE HDF5 products is described [<span class="underline">in the Metadata section</span> <span class="underline">of this site</span>](https://sar.iceye.com/5.2.0/productFormats/metadata/). See more information about the format in [<span class="underline">the HDF Group’s own website</span>](https://www.hdfgroup.org/solutions/hdf5/).

The HDF5 is excellent for many expert users in the science and military intelligence communities and those who are familiar with SAR specific software tools such as SNAP and SARScape. However, as was alluded above, in the 2010s the GeoTIFF container format and especially its newer version, the Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF (COG), have become more popular as they have helped make SLC imagery accessible with a broader range of tools, and for a broader range of users and industries.

For example, even the general GIS software tool QGIS can directly exploit the amplitude data from SLCs packaged into GeoTIFF containers, because the two components, amplitude and phase, are stored as separate bands within the file similar to how multispectral images use the container format. Moreover, the COG format enables faster and more efficient visualization and analysis especially when the SLC files are stored in the cloud.

To provide full metadata, GeoTIFFs come with a sidecar file that is increasingly provided in the GeoJSON format. The metadata provides essential information about the acquisition geometry, calibration, and processing parameters. Many GIS tools can automatically utilize the RPC values (i.e. geocoding) in the metadata to perform automatic ground projection, so they are ready to be used as part of analysis and exploitation workflows.

### 10.2.4) Applications and Use Cases

The phase data recorded by SAR imaging enables the many techniques and applications listed in Sections <span class="underline">9.7</span> and <span class="underline">9.8</span>, of which InSAR and CCD are especially popular, in addition to all the amplitude-based techniques listed in <span class="underline">9.6</span>. This is why the SLC is sought after in fields as diverse as climate science, geophysics, and structural engineering. SLC images are also often used by users who prefer to have full control over the processing steps of multi-looking, detection and projection.

### 10.2.5) Advantages and Limitations

SLC images offer unmatched resolution, fidelity and phase coherence, retain all the original sensor measurements and are free from interpolation artifacts or projection issues, making them ideal for advanced analysis and custom workflows as well as for further processing into more refined data products. Users can perform orthorectification themselves using the digital elevation or surface model that they deem provides the geospatial accuracy that serves their analytical needs.

However, the large size of SLCs can present challenges in storage and processing especially when large-scale datasets are required. Working with SLC products also requires modern GIS and/or SAR tools and expertise, as SLCs almost always require further interpretation and processing due to the slant range geometry and other characteristics, and leveraging the phase data requires SAR-specific analytics features.

## 10.3) Sensor Independent Complex and Derived Data (SICD & SIDD)

<span class="underline">Sensor Independent Complex Data (SICD)</span> and <span class="underline">Sensor Independent Derived Data (SIDD)</span> are highly structured and standardized formats designed to enable straightforward advanced exploitation of SAR data irrespective of the SAR sensor system, collection strategy, or processing algorithms used in data collection and preparation. The SICD and SIDD refer to two different products although they are almost always delivered together: SICD provides the full complex SAR data and is thus similar to the SLC, while SIDD provides a more processed and application-ready amplitude-only data product, somewhat similar to the GRD.

### 10.3.1 Origins and Purpose

The SICD and SIDD standards emerged from collaborative efforts among government, defense, and research organizations in the early 2000s, with the SICD and SIDD specifications being formally published by the US National Geospatial Intelligence Agency (NGA) in 2011 and 2015, respectively. The purpose was to create a unified framework for SAR data representation that would enable seamless integration of data from diverse SAR platforms into various analytic workflows. In other words, the intention was that any algorithm designed to process data in the SICD format would work on data from any SAR vendor without any special adaptations. SIDD was thereafter developed to cater to users who required data products with specific processing steps already applied, such as multi-looking, radiometric calibration, and geocoding, while still also being sensor-independent.

While SLC data products existed and were widely used, the SICD standard arose to address limitations in interoperability and standardization. SLC formats were often sensor-specific, making them cumbersome in multi-platform workflows. SICD resolved this issue by introducing a format that ensured consistency and usability across diverse systems, enhancing the potential for collaborative and cross-platform exploitation.

### 10.3.2) Processing and Preparation

SICD data products retain raw complex SAR data, including amplitude and phase information, in a sensor-independent format. This data undergoes essential preprocessing steps, such as motion compensation, radiometric calibration, and geometric correction, while remaining in slant range geometry. These processes are similar to those for SLC products but with a critical distinction: SICD emphasizes standardization and detailed metadata for consistent interpretation across platforms.

SIDD products, by contrast, involve additional processing to generate application-ready imagery and are thus level 2 data products. Key steps include multi-looking to reduce speckle noise, projection to ground range or map coordinates for easier visualization, and radiometric corrections to standardize backscatter values. This makes SIDD data directly usable for a wide range of applications without requiring specialized SAR expertise which makes them a valuable complement to the SICD, much like the relationship between the GRD and the SLC.

### 10.3.3) Formats and Packaging

SICD products are typically stored in the National Imagery Transmission Format (NITF) which is a versatile and metadata-rich container that ensures data integrity and compatibility with advanced SAR processing tools. NITF allows for embedding both the image data and comprehensive metadata, covering aspects such as acquisition geometry, sensor parameters, and radiometric calibration details. This structure enables sophisticated exploitation and analysis of SICD data.

SIDD products may be packaged together with the SICD in the same NITF container, enabling seamless access to raw and derived data within a single package. This arrangement enhances usability by providing users with both foundational and application-ready data in a cohesive format.

However, SIDD products are frequently provided as separate files in formats like GeoTIFF or Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF (COG) for broader accessibility. When delivered in these formats, SIDD data often includes accompanying metadata sidecar files in XML or JSON formats, detailing the processing steps applied and the parameters used. This packaging facilitates integration with general-purpose GIS tools while maintaining transparency about the transformations applied to the data.

Note that NGA has released a range of libraries in common environments (including MATLAB, Python and C/C++) so that tools and algorithms can convert a wide range of SLC formats into SICD and can use SICD files for SAR sensor-independent analysis and visualization.

### 10.3.4) Applications and Use Cases

SICD and SIDD data products serve a wide array of applications. SICD products can be applied to the same advanced SAR exploitation techniques where SLCs can, so all of the use cases described in Section [<span class="underline">10.2.4</span>](#applications-and-use-cases-1) apply. The standardized sensor-independent format provided by SICD can be critical for some users’ applications, where data from multiple SAR platforms is utilized.

The use cases for SIDD products are similar to those for the GRD which are listed in Section [<span class="underline">10.4.4</span>](#applications-and-use-cases-3), including environmental monitoring, flood mapping, disaster response, infrastructure assessment, and urban planning. While SLC and SICD products can also support these applications, the SIDD’s application-ready format and its better compatibility with also non-SAR oriented GIS tools makes it accessible to and preferred by a wider user base.

### 10.3.5) Advantages and Limitations

The primary advantage of the SICD and SIDD data products is that they allow users to mix data from multiple vendors and their varying SAR imaging platforms with minimal issues. The complementarity of SICD and SIDD products in the same bundle also ensures that users can select the most suitable dataset for their needs: SICD powers even the most advanced analyses, while SIDD delivers convenience and ease of use for immediate application.

However, the complexity and large size of the bundle files can be excessive for users with limited computational resources and expertise in SAR data exploitation. Such users might prefer the SLC or an amplitude-only data product such as the GRD.

## 10.4) Ground Range Detected (GRD) Images

<span class="underline">Ground Range Detected (GRD) images</span> are amplitude images that are projected into the ground range plane, and are usually (depending on imaging mode) multi-looked to reduce speckle noise and achieve square pixels. The GRD is widely recognized for its interpretability and compatibility with conventional geospatial tools, which is why it has become a staple in many operational and research contexts, and in applications ranging from environmental monitoring to disaster response.

### 10.4.1) Origins and Purpose

The development of GRD images stems from the need to simplify SAR data for users who do not require the complex processing or information content associated with raw or phase-preserved SAR products. While complex data products are mandatory for many specialized analyses, there is a large variety of analyses where phase data is not used. GRD bridges this gap by offering images that retain the essential backscatter information needed for a wide range of popular applications.

The GRD became well standardized and globally known almost in tandem with the SLC in the 1990s, because data from many of the same pioneering SAR missions were used to produce both data products for different applications. Indeed, the role of GRDs continued to strengthen, and the early 2000s brought the first SAR missions, such as the ENVISAT ASAR (2002) and ALOS PALSAR (2006), that provided GRDs as the default product.

### 10.4.2) Processing and Preparation

The phase component of the radar signal is always discarded in GRD detection, leaving only the amplitude information. Multi-looking is performed to reduce speckle and improve visual interpretability. However, not all GRDs are multilooked, as some SAR systems are able to provide symmetric IPR (see Section [<span class="underline">6.4</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md#64-spatial-resolution-and-impulse-response)) directly with certain collection strategies. For example, ICEYE is able to provide single-look Strip and ScanSAR collections with approximately symmetric IPR, moderate resolution and somewhat low speckle due to advanced radar design, hardware and processing. For this reason, Strip, Scan, and Scan Wide images are not multilooked.

Second, the data is projected to the ground plane using an Earth ellipsoid model (EEM). For this projection, a standard reference ellipsoid (such as the WGS84) is used to determine an averaged fixed value of terrain height for the whole scene. Note that by default the images are arranged in the shadows-down sample arrangement that is an artifact-free standard choice in SAR.

An optional step is radiometric terrain correction (RTC), which can be performed to improve the consistency and comparability of backscatter intensity values across the image and across acquisitions. This involves using the EEM to calculate and account for the effect of the varying incidence angle and range within the scene on backscatter intensity, explained in more detail in Article [<span class="underline">7</span>](../techdocs/07_radiometric_considerations.md) of the Technical Documentation.

The GRD is not an orthorectified product. Instead of providing pre-orthorectified image data products, ICEYE geocodes and georeferences the images and provides both rational polynomial coefficients (RPCs) and any ground control points (GCPs) in the metadata along with guidance on how users can orthorectify their own images using their own DEMs or DSMs. This approach ensures users understand the advantages and limitations of the orthorectification of their imagery and are able to make optimizations as necessary.

### 10.4.3) Formats and Packaging

GRD images are typically delivered in the GeoTIFF format, which is widely supported by geospatial analysis software. Accompanying metadata is often embedded within the GeoTIFF file or provided as separate XML or JSON files, detailing acquisition parameters, radiometric calibration constants, and georeferencing information. Some leading providers also offer GRD products in cloud-optimized GeoTIFF (COG) format, enabling efficient streaming and processing in cloud-based environments.

The use of these standardized formats ensures that GRD products are interoperable across diverse software platforms, including both open-source tools and proprietary systems, and explains the popularity and utility of GRD products in operational workflows.

### 10.4.4) Applications and Use Cases

GRD images are utilized in a diverse array of domains due to their user-friendly nature and practical utility. They are extensively employed in environmental monitoring, where they support tasks like tracking changes in vegetation cover, flooding, and land degradation. Their preprocessed format makes them invaluable during disaster response operations, enabling rapid insights into flood extents, earthquake damage, and other natural hazards.

In agriculture, GRD images facilitate monitoring of crop health, land use optimization, and irrigation planning. Urban planners and infrastructure managers leverage GRD data to assess changes in built environments, monitor construction activities, and evaluate damage to critical infrastructure. Additionally, GRD products are widely used in marine and coastal applications, including ship detection, oil spill monitoring, and the study of coastal erosion.

See Section <span class="underline">9.7</span> for a description of amplitude-based techniques and applications, as the GRD data product can be leveraged for all of them.

### 10.4.5) Advantages and Limitations

The primary advantage of GRD images lies in their accessibility and ease of use. Their amplitude-only nature speeds up data transfer, reduces memory consumption, and simplifies interpretation, making them suitable for a broad audience, including non-specialists. The georeferenced GeoTIFF format and radiometric calibration ensure that GRD products are directly compatible with widely used GIS and remote sensing tools, streamlining integration into existing workflows. The availability of the COG format further enhances the utility of GRDs in cloud-based applications.

However, GRD products have limitations. The lack of phase data restricts their applicability in advanced analyses such as interferometry or coherent change detection. Additionally, the multi-looking process, while improving visual clarity, results in some loss of spatial resolution. These limitations are considered features in many applications, which is why the GRD remains one of the most popular SAR data product types.

## 10.5) Colorized Sub-Aperture Stack Images (CSI)

A <span class="underline">Colorized Sub-Aperture Stack Image (CSI)</span> is a specialized level 2 SAR data product that utilizes multiple sub-aperture images to create a composite representation of a scene. In a CSI, the long synthetic aperture of a single Spotlight collection is divided to produce a series of colorized images of the same scene, each with slightly different angular and temporal perspective. The output is a colorful composite image that makes detecting certain scatterers and motion immediately clear.

The CSI concept builds upon traditional SAR sub-aperture processing techniques but introduces a new approach by capturing differences in radar reflectivity at varying look angles and points in time, and mapping these variations to different color channels, offering richer visual cues about surface properties and structural features.

### 10.5.1) Origins and Purpose

The CSI data product was first introduced publicly in 2020 by ICEYE along with the SAR Video product that is introduced in Section [<span class="underline">10.6</span>](#106-sar-video-vid). Traditional SAR products, such as SLC and GRD, combine all available aperture data into a single image to maximize resolution and image quality. However, this approach does not highlight potential information contained in individual sub-apertures. The CSI was developed to capture and represent these additional insights.

CSI products enable <span class="underline">aspect-dependent scattering analysis</span>: The different colors reveal incidence angle-dependent reflectivity or normalized radar cross-section (NRCS) variations, helping analysts distinguish between human-made objects and natural terrain. Figure [<span class="underline">10.3</span>](#figure-10.3-a-csi-image-highlighting-human-made-objects-in-a-forest) provides an example of a CSI composite image in which man-made vehicles and infrastructure stand out in various colors amid the mostly grayscale forest.

Human-made objects tend to stand out because they typically consist of flat, linear surfaces joined together at sharp angles. At some incidence angles, these features scatter energy away from the sensor and can look quite dark, while at other angles reflect energy very strongly. Across the sub-apertures that compose a full SAR image, there is often at least one sub-aperture where a part of the structure glints most brightly, and thus that respective color dominates in the composite image. In contrast, for many natural objects, such as the trees in Figure [<span class="underline">10.3</span>](#figure-10.3-a-csi-image-highlighting-human-made-objects-in-a-forest), the backscatter values are very similar across all of the sub-apertures leading to the different colors co-adding into a shade of grey, and thereby fading into the background in the composite image.

Since each sub-aperture is systematically colored from a specific color scale (red at the beginning of the collection, blue at the end), the pixel colors in the composite image directly denote the imaging geometry at which the backscatter was strongest. This information can be used to identify and characterize scatterers in the scene.

The CSI enables <span class="underline">dwell-time motion analysis</span> as the different colors reveal temporal dynamics and motion. Figure [<span class="underline">10.4</span>](#figure-10.4-example-of-colorized-sub-aperture-image-csi-of-an-airport-highlighting-human-made-structures-and-equipment-in-dominant-colors) illustrates how the different images’ colors clearly expose fast moving targets, such as motor vehicles as illustrated in the figure. Since the sub-aperture images are taken at a known, constant rate, it is possible to calculate direction of movement as well as object speed and acceleration.

### 10.5.2) Processing and Preparation

CSI production picks up where CPHD production (see Section [<span class="underline">10.1.2</span>](#processing-and-preparation)) left off. For best results the phase history data (PHD) should be collected with the Spotlight strategy with a long (\>10 s) imaging period to provide good angular and temporal variability.

After the corrections, compensations and calibrations are performed on the collected PHD, the data is split into multiple overlapping sub-apertures that represent continuous portions of the full synthetic aperture arc. Each sub-aperture corresponds to a slightly different azimuth look angle. The first sub-aperture contains the first imaging data while the satellite was still approaching the scene, and the last the data that was collected somewhat equally far after having already passed the scene.

Second, each sub-aperture dataset is focused and processed into a distinct amplitude image following the same processing steps as in GRD image production (see [<span class="underline">10.4.2</span>](#processing-and-preparation-3)). However, further radiometric normalization, geometric alignment, and temporal co-registration (see Section [<span class="underline">6.5</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md#65-co-registration)) is performed to ensure consistency across the whole sub-aperture image stack.

Third, each sub-aperture image is individually colored typically with a colormap that spans from red to blue, such that the earliest images in the series are colored with various shades of red and the last images with various shades of blue. The particular colormap chosen is arbitrary, and provides insight on scattering properties regardless of the colormap used. See Figure [<span class="underline">10.5</span>](#figure-10.5-animation-of-csi-sub-aperture-splits-and-colorization.) for an animated illustration.

Fourth, a composite image is formed by coadding all the distinctly colorized sub-aperture images.

Finally, since CSI is a level 2 or derivative data product intended for direct human interpretation, the composite image may undergo contrast enhancement, filtering, or other raster image processing steps to improve visual interpretability. Figures <span class="underline">10.3</span> and <span class="underline">10.4</span> in the preceding subsection showcase crops of finished CSI products.

### 10.5.3) Formats and Packaging

CSI products are commonly delivered in standard image formats such as the GeoTIFF, depending on whether geospatial referencing is required. Metadata typically accompanies the image, describing the sub-aperture processing parameters and encoding methodology. Given their visual nature, CSI products are often used in conjunction with traditional SAR data products to enhance situational awareness.

ICEYE delivers CSI files as GeoTIFFs with not just the composite image but also each individual sub-aperture image included independently as separate bands within the container, together with full metadata including the exact time, duration, and satellite location for each sub-aperture.

### 10.5.4) Applications and Use Cases

CSI products have a growing number of applications, particularly in reconnaissance, surveillance, and infrastructure assessment. While traditional SAR images often require further processing and expert interpretation to discern subtle scattering differences, CSIs enhance situational awareness by visually encoding changes in reflectivity and motion. CSI images can reveal human-made objects amid vegetation, helping distinguish between different scattering mechanisms, structures, materials and terrains. For that reason, CSIs also have utility for quick building damage assessment - damaged structures often have returns that differentiate them from in-tact structures, making the CSI useful for post-wildfire or post- earthquake damage assessment in built up areas.

### 10.5.5) Advantages and Limitations

CSIs offer a unique opportunity to quickly and easily leverage multi-aspect and multi-temporal analyses from a single-pass SAR data collection. CSI products bring out human-made structures even amid somewhat dense vegetation, as well as moving objects within a scene. The colorization scheme empowers intuitive human interpretation and allows rough estimation of scatterer structures as well as movement, relative acceleration and velocity, and directions of travel.

However, CSI products do not replace level 1 data products nor traditional SAR analysis methods. Instead they are best used in conjunction with other data products. CSI images lack phase data altogether and the amplitude data resolutions and signal-to-noise ratios are lower for a CSI than for a comparable SLC or GRD product.

## 10.6) SAR Video (VID)

A <span class="underline">SAR Video (VID)</span> is a specialized level 2 SAR data product where data from a single Spotlight collection is divided and focused into a series of sub-aperture images that are then organized as a sequence of video frames, thereby explicitly extending traditional static SAR imagery into the time domain.

The VID is similar to the CSI in that both products split the long synthetic aperture of a collection to analyze individual frames of the same scene. The VID product is neither colorized nor co-added to create a composite image, but the product shares similar benefits and trade-offs, and supports similar applications and use cases.

When played as a video, the different sub-aperture frames of VID products highlight the angular dependency of scattering properties of certain structures, and capture motion of objects. In some cases with moving objects, it can be easier to identify an object from a single, lower resolution frame of the collection rather than the full resolution image.

### 10.6.1) Origins and Purpose

ICEYE pioneered both the CSI and the VID products and launched them to the public in tandem in 2020. Both products leverage the sub-apertures and imaging durations of long Spotlight collections (Dwell, Dwell Fine, Dwell Precise) to enable multi-aspect and multi-temporal SAR imaging, instead of maximizing resolution and overall image quality.

### 10.6.2) Processing and Preparation

SAR Video and CSI production are identical over multiple steps: Both are generated using Dwell mode images (Dwell, Dwell Fine, Dwell Precise) which use a 25 second imaging window and the Spotlight collection strategy. Compensations are applied to the collected phase history data, after which the data is subdivided and focused into independent sub-aperture images. Each image is then further adjusted to ensure consistency within the stack. Refer to Section [<span class="underline">10.5</span>](#105-colorized-sub-aperture-stack-images-csi) for more detailed walk-through of these shared steps as they are only summarized here.

At this point the CSI and VID production processes diverge: Instead of applying the CSI colorization scheme, in VID production the images are kept monocolor and simply organized as a chronological sequence of frames in a video stream.

Finally, simple raster image and video enhancements could be applied as a form of polishing, since the VID product is a level two derivative product intended for direct human interpretation. Figures [<span class="underline">10.6</span>](#figure-10.6-cropped-sar-video-featuring-moving-vessels) and [<span class="underline">10.7</span>](#figure-10.7-cropped-sar-video-featuring-waves-and-glinting-from-human-made-objects) in the preceding subsection showcase crops of finished VID products packaged into the GIF format to maximize compatibility across web browsers.

### 10.6.3) Formats and Packaging

SAR Video products are typically packaged with the MPEG4 video file format (MP4). Some providers complement the delivery with a low resolution GIF file to provide an accessible sneak peek of the main product.

Additionally, a file in the GeoTIFF format may be also included where all of the sub-aperture images are available as separate bands to facilitate frame by frame analysis using a geospatial image data exploitation tool. This allows the user to measure and track changes and moving objects between frames by stepping through the different bands of the image. ICEYE embeds full metadata for each of the video frames in the GeoTIFF.

### 10.6.4) Applications and Use Cases

SAR Video has similarly diverse applications as the CSI (see [<span class="underline">10.5.4</span>](#applications-and-use-cases-4)): understanding surface features and how they change throughout a collection period. SAR Video can be used to identify moving targets such as vehicles and vessels, observe objects under some types of tree cover, and understand angular and temporal variations,dependencies, and characteristics of scatterers.

### 10.6.5) Advantages and Limitations

The key advantage of the VID product is that it combines both the multi-aspect and multi-temporal nature of sub-aperture imagery into a video format. The VID is a powerful complement to the SAR product portfolio which would otherwise consist of only static imagery products. Like the CSI, VID products are unparalleled in facilitating the analysis of human-made structures and moving objects within a single radar image collection. However, SAR Video products are not a substitute for level 1 data products such as the SLC or GRD. On the contrary, VID products are best used to complement other data products, by speeding up and enriching data analysis related to decision-making in fast-paced operations.
