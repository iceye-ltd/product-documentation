# 8\) SAR Imagery & Interpretation Considerations

!!! summary inline end

    - SAR pixels are complex numbers with both amplitude and phase components; amplitude or backscatter intensity images must be "detected" from the complex data, while techniques like interferometry and change detection leverage phase data for high-precision analysis.

    - Backscatter variability stems from multiple factors: polarization choices affect sensitivity to different surface orientations; wavelength determines penetration depth; scatterer structure drives the dominant scattering mechanism; and moisture content strongly influences reflectivity.

    - Three primary scattering mechanisms produce distinct signatures in SAR imagery: specular reflection from smooth surfaces creates dark areas with low NRCS; double-bounce from vertical structures and ground creates very bright returns; and volumetric scattering from rough or vegetated surfaces produces moderate backscatter values.

    - Geometric distortions are inherent to side-looking SAR geometry and cannot be fully avoided: foreshortening compresses terrain slopes that face the radar, layover inverts tall features toward the sensor, and radar shadow creates no-data regions behind obstructions—all varying with the imaging geometry and topography.

    - Speckle is not noise but rather an interference pattern resulting from coherent summation of returns from multiple scatterers within each resolution cell; it can be reduced through multi-looking or filtering, though at the cost of spatial resolution.

    - Image quality metrics approach SAR performance from complementary angles: RNIIRS provides subjective, task-based interpretability ratings requiring expert evaluation, while RGIQE offers an objective, physics-based measure of information content per unit area derived from bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio, balancing resolution against noise performance.

A SAR sensor does not take a picture the way a camera does. Instead, it measures the microwave echoes produced by the ground surface that it illuminates and then digitally processes this data into a high resolution representation in range and azimuth. The data is then processed into different levels of products to optimize their usefulness for specific applications. The processing flow and common options are described in Chapter [<span class="underline">9</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md) and the main data product types are described in Chapter [<span class="underline">10</span>](10_data_products.md). Before we discuss the details of SAR processing, this section presents theoretical basics relating to SAR images – especially the concepts of complex, detected, phase and amplitude images – and dives deeper into the factors that impact backscatter, the genesis of geometric distortions and the concept of speckle – all of which are important to understand when interpreting most if not all SAR imagery data products. It concludes by exploring measures and metrics used to represent SAR image quality.

## 8.1) Complex and Amplitude Images

Unlike an optical image, each pixel in a SAR image is represented by a complex number. The concept of complex numbers is adopted from mathematics where complex numbers are numbers with two components: a real and an imaginary component. In SAR, the two components are amplitude and phase. The <span class="underline">amplitude component</span> is a measure of the amount of radar energy reflected back to the sensor. The <span class="underline">phase component</span> is a measure of the distance that the echoed wave signal has traveled relative to the transmitted signal within a wave cycle (remember Figure [<span class="underline">6.1</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md#figure-61-periodic-wave-terminology)). For more information on amplitude and phase, please see Article [<span class="underline">6</span>](../techdocs/06_the_quadrature_representation_of_complex_data.md) in the Technical Documentation.

Figure [<span class="underline">8.1</span>](#figure-8.1-amplitude-and-phase-components-of-a-sar-image) illustrates both the amplitude and phase components of a complex image. The image on the left represents the amplitude component <span class="underline"> </span> of a scene with a single real-valued brightness value assigned to each pixel. The image on the right represents the phase component of the same scene with each pixel representing the corresponding phase component values. The combination of these two components is called a <span class="underline">complex image</span>, in which every pixel has both amplitude and phase values.

<figcaption><strong>Figure 8.1: Amplitude and Phase Components of a SAR Image.</strong> SAR data collection results in "complex" data which includes both amplitude and phase values for each pixel and which thereby can be visualized separately. Here both the amplitude and phase values are visualized for a small scene featuring agricultural fields.</figcaption>

To clarify terminology it must be noted that the term <span class="underline">detected image</span> is often used as a synonym of the term *amplitude image* – i.e. an image with just the amplitude data – and the term *detection* as the process of computing just the amplitude values. This is because traditionally, the majority of radar data users have often only been interested in the amplitude data and have thus skipped storing and even computing the phase-offset values during detection (the conversion from the I/Q domain that is explained in Section [FIXME: Link text/name<span class="underline">X.X</span>](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1cJWcLfcTurrisVG9ehbDK9fdocGlJ62uCY_PaBX6xoE/edit?pli=1&tab=t.0#heading=h.nb25ei6nn80u)<span class="underline"></span> in the Technical Documentation). Due to this history, amplitude images are still often erroneously considered the only or primary type of data product that SAR can provide, but in reality they offer only half of what SAR data records, and represent only one category of SAR data products.

While amplitude data can be used directly for human interpretation, phase data may seem initially useless to a human interpreter. However, it encodes powerful information and has several interesting use cases. Phase pixels are a unique and valuable product of radar and SAR imaging: Phase data can be used to manipulate the synthetic aperture in different ways to extract useful information that is not available from amplitude images. Moreover, changes in the phase measurements of the same object in different images can be used to detect small surface structure characteristics, such as tire tracks on gravel or millimeter scale ground displacement. We will explore these products in chapter 11.

## 8.2) Backscatter, Polarization and Scattering Mechanisms

We have discussed how a synthetic aperture can be used to collect high resolution data. When a SAR antenna transmits pulses of energy to a surface, not all the energy returns to the sensor. Some of the energy penetrates the surface, some is absorbed by the surface, and some of those emitted pulses scatter away from the sensor. Only the scattered energy which returns to the sensor is called <span class="underline">backscatter</span>. Generally, the term is a synonym of <span class="underline">single-bounce backscatter</span>, which is the radar signal energy that is reflected 180° from the very first incidence with a scatterer. However, scattered signal pulses may also return to the radar sensor after multiple reflections, which is called <span class="underline">double-</span> or <span class="underline">multi-bounce scattering</span>, or through other scattering phenomena, some of which are described and illustrated further below (Figure [<span class="underline">8.3</span>](#figure-8.3-three-primary-scattering-mechanisms)).

The radar reflectivity measure Normalized Radar Cross Section (NRCS) or radar backscatter coefficient (sigma nought), covered in Section [<span class="underline">7.4</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md#74-noise-equivalent-sigma-zero-and-the-radar-cross-section), is useful in comparing surfaces and scattering phenomena. In the following paragraphs, we will discuss various factors that explain variability in backscatter and thus NRCS values, including signal polarization and wavelength, scatterer structure and moisture content, and imaging geometry.

<span class="underline">Polarization</span>, illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">8.2</span>](#figure-8.2-a-vertically-polarized-electromagnetic-plane-wave), can be defined as the orientation of the electric field vector of the plane wave that carries the electromagnetic signal energy. The polarization of transmitted radar signals is determined by the antenna's physical type, geometry and orientation, as well as other system components, such as any waveguides, feed networks and polarization control mechanisms. These also influence signal reception: A system optimized for a specific polarization captures signals with a misaligned polarization suboptimally or not at all.

<figure id="figure-8.2-a-vertically-polarized-electromagnetic-plane-wave">
<img src="https://github.com/sjjsy/product-documentation/releases/download/additional-assets-test/image33.png">
<figcaption><strong>Figure 8.2: A Vertically Polarized Electromagnetic Plane Wave.</strong> The plane wave with wavelength λ has its electric field vector E (red) oscillating in the vertical direction. The magnetic field B is always at right angles to it (blue), and both are perpendicular to the direction of propagation (z).</figcaption>
</figure>

Commonly recognized and used polarization combinations in SAR are the co-polarized <span class="underline">VV</span>: transmit vertical, receive vertical; and <span class="underline">HH</span>: transmit horizontal, receive horizontal; and the cross-polarized <span class="underline">VH</span>: transmit vertical, receive horizontal; and <span class="underline">HV</span>: transmit horizontal, receive vertical. Circular and other polarizations also exist, but are less commonly employed.

These different combinations, used individually or together, enable SAR sensors to collect data about the polarimetric properties of the surface, namely its structure and orientation. The polarization used to illuminate an area will impact the final appearance of the image. Vertical objects, such as tall buildings, reflect vertical waves strongly. In comparison, horizontal waves reflect well from horizontal objects and surfaces such as water bodies and hanging overhead power lines. This means an image collected with VV polarization will look different than one collected with HH polarization, even if nothing on the ground surface changed.

Polarization choices are important in system design: For example, ICEYE relies on <span class="underline">Single Polarization (Single-Pol)</span> VV sensors, which can be considered optimal for maritime applications due to their sensitivity for elements such as ships and even their wakes or traces breaking the smooth flat water surfaces, crop analysis due to their sensitivity to vegetation structure, as well as urban monitoring and the detection of human-made targets under foliage due to their sensitivity to sharp features.

Conversely, HH polarization may be preferred when lesser sensitivity for surface roughness is desired, such as in dry terrain monitoring, certain types of agricultural monitoring, and glacier monitoring; and the cross-polarization modes HV and VH may be preferred for forest analysis, soil moisture measurement and target classification, for example.

A SAR system can be designed with two (<span class="underline">Dual-Pol</span>) or four polarization channels (<span class="underline">Quad-Pol</span>) by using active phased array antennas (introduced in Section [<span class="underline">4.1</span>](04_antennas_and_sar_geometry.md#41-active-phased-array-antennas-boresight-and-gain)) with dual-polarized patch elements or dipoles that are capable of transmitting and/or receiving two orthogonal polarization states, either simultaneously or in alternating pulses. Dual-Pol systems typically transmit in one while receiving both co- and cross-polarized returns (VV+VH or HH+HV), while Quad-Pol systems can both transmit and receive both horizontal and vertical polarizations.

Relative to Single-Pol systems, Dual- and Quad-Pol systems are more complex and may necessitate reduced illumination and receipt power due to the division of transmission and receipt capacity across polarizations- a possible result of this is reduced swath sizes as well as lower resolution and signal-to-noise performance. Therefore, most commercial SAR systems have opted for Single-Pol as a sensible choice for most use cases.

After tackling polarization, we now move to the second fundamental signal property that should be emphasized in relation to backscatter: <span class="underline">wavelength</span>. In Chapter [<span class="underline">3</span>](03_radar_frequency_bands.md), we discussed how the wavelength of the electromagnetic radiation (EMR) emitted by a sensor determines how the ground surface will scatter and reflect energy. In practice this means that the exact same area of Earth will look different in an X-band image than it will in an L-band image. A good rule of thumb is that the longer the wavelength emitted by a SAR system, the greater the depth of penetration. For example, over a forest with dense canopy, the backscatter from X-band waves will primarily be the result of interactions with the top of the canopy, while backscatter from L-band waves would likely form from interactions with the forest floor, as illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">3.1</span>](03_radar_frequency_bands.md#figure-31-differences-in-penetration-capacity)netration-capacity.-this-figure-illustrates-how-three-commonly-used-wavelength-bands-for-sar-interact-differently-with-different-surface-features.).

The third major factor determining backscatter is scatterer structure: Trees, gravel, paved roads, and buildings all interact differently with microwaves due to their roughness relative to the emitted wavelength, and their structure and orientation relative to the sensor. Three primary scattering mechanisms are illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">8.3</span>](#figure-8.3-three-primary-scattering-mechanisms), namely specular reflection, volumetric scattering, and double bounce.

<figure id="figure-8.3-three-primary-scattering-mechanisms">
<img src="https://github.com/sjjsy/product-documentation/releases/download/additional-assets-test/image34.png">
<figcaption><strong>Figure 8.3: Three Primary Scattering Mechanisms.</strong></figcaption>
</figure>

<span class="underline">Specular reflection</span> manifests in smooth surfaces, such as calm water and paved roads, that tend to reflect energy away from the sensor, resulting in low backscatter values and thus dark areas in a radar image. Such smooth surfaces are therefore associated with low NRCS values.

The <span class="underline">double-bounce</span> mechanism manifests when a radar pulse first interacts with a vertical surface, such as a building or a tree trunk, then reflects off a secondary surface, such as the pavement or ground, before returning to the sensor. This results in high backscatter and NRCS values, producing very bright areas in a radar image. This is also known as <span class="underline">corner reflection</span> and together with specular reflection can cause strong returns in images of urban scenes, due to the geometry and structure of such environments.

!!! note
    Certain urban structures, bridges, cables and metallic assemblies can give rise to <span class="underline">triple-bounce</span> and even higher-order (multi-bounce) scattering. Such returns are usually weaker because each successive reflection incurs energy loss, and complex multi-bounce paths may also interfere destructively. For this reason, triple-bounce is the most commonly observed higher-order mechanism in practice, while returns beyond three bounces are possible but rarer.

<span class="underline">Volumetric scattering</span> manifests in rough surfaces, such as trees and other vegetation but also snow, that tend to somewhat randomly scatter energy in all directions, resulting in moderate backscatter and NRCS values and somewhat bright areas in a radar image. This is also called <span class="underline">diffuse scattering</span> and surfaces that manifest this are called <span class="underline">diffuse surfaces</span>.

The fourth key factor influencing backscatter is the material's electrical conductivity and relative permittivity (dielectric constant), both of which are strongly affected by its moisture content. Typical SAR wavelengths reflect off metals and other good conductors of electricity within the first micrometer of penetration depth. With the right incidence angle, the backscatter can be very strong. However, the interaction of EMR with poor conductors tends to be weak, unless those conductors are moist. This relates to a physical quantity called <span class="underline">relative permittivity</span>, also known as dielectric constant, which is a measure of the electric polarizability of a dielectric material, or insulator, relative to vacuum.

The moisture content of many materials, such as soil and vegetation, strongly affects their relative permittivity: The higher the water content, the stronger the backscatter. For example, dry soil and vegetation typically appear darker than when they are wet. Consequently, moisture levels can also be inferred based on backscatter brightness, which is another reason radar imagery can be powerful in forestry, agriculture and wetland monitoring.

Lastly, one must consider <span class="underline">imaging geometry</span> when interpreting a SAR image. For example, backscatter returns from smooth surfaces are stronger at low incidence angles (approaching vertical), but have low returns at higher incidence angles (approaching horizontal). Imaging geometry also impacts geometric effects like shadows, foreshortening, and layover, which we will cover in the next section.

## 8.3) Geometric Distortions

Geometric distortions and effects are inherent to SAR imagery and unavoidable due to the side-looking nature of the sensor. The primary distortions are foreshortening, layover and shadow. These are illustrated in Figures [<span class="underline">8.4</span>](#figure-8.4-the-most-relevant-geometric-distortions-affecting-sar-imagery) and [<span class="underline">8.5</span>](#figure-8.5-iceye-sar-images-illustrating-distortions) and described further below. These distortions are dependent on look angle, position in the near or far range, and topography and are a consequence of the SAR imaging principles.

As these distortions are inherent to SAR imagery (remember, a radar measures electromagnetic response as a function of range), the best way to mitigate them is to carefully consider the topography and surface features when determining the imaging geometry of a SAR collection.

<img src="https://github.com/sjjsy/product-documentation/releases/download/additional-assets-test/image36.png">
<figcaption><strong>Figure 8.5: ICEYE SAR Images Illustrating Distortions.</strong> From left to right, these images illustrate the distortions called foreshortening, layover, and shadow. In each case, the satellite projects the beam top-down relative to the 2D image.</figcaption>
</figure>

<span class="underline">Foreshortening</span> occurs when the radar beam reaches the bottom of a target before the top of a target, causing the target to appear compressed. This is illustrated in the leftmost image of Figure [<span class="underline">8.5</span>](#figure-8.5-iceye-sar-images-illustrating-distortions), where it seems as if the sensor- or top-facing (and bright) sides of the hills are much steeper and shorter than the bottom-facing (and dark) sides, when in reality they are somewhat equally steep. This compression intensifies as the terrain slope angle rises and as local incidence angle approaches zero. When it is close to zero, backscatter from both the top and bottom of the slope arrive at the receiver almost at the same time, rendering slope features compressed in range and brighter due to the concentrated backscatter. When the local incidence angle is almost exactly zero across the slope, the radar cannot distinguish range differences across points in the slope (the A' and B' in Figure [<span class="underline">8.4</span>](#figure-8.4-the-most-relevant-geometric-distortions-affecting-sar-imagery) are at the same point in the slant range line) and it shows as fully compressed, and thus almost invisible in the image. Fortunately, foreshortening effects can be somewhat corrected with a DEM.

Foreshortening effects decrease as look angle increases. This is because when the look angle increases, the imaging perspective becomes more side-looking, and more slant range is available to measure the slopes facing the radar. It might help to think of the opposite case: at small look angles the satellite is closer to nadir and is looking at slopes more head-on, which can cause those slopes to be more compressed in the resulting image.

!!! note
    Remember that look angle, the angle between the radar's nadir and the radar beam, is equal to incidence angle, the angle between the incident beam and the local surface normal, only if we assume a flat Earth model with no Earth curvature and no variation in ground elevation. However, in reality, the local incidence angles can vary greatly across parts of the terrain even when look angle remains constant.

<span class="underline">Layover</span> occurs when the radar signal reaches the top of a target before the base of a target, inverting its geographic position. This is illustrated by the "veils of layover" of the tall buildings in the center image in Figure [<span class="underline">8.5</span>](#figure-8.5-iceye-sar-images-illustrating-distortions) and also in Figure [<span class="underline">8.6</span>](#figure-8.6-layover-on-mt-fuji) where the peak of Mt. Fuji is "laid over" the terrain ahead of it towards the sensor. Because of layover, tall structures seemingly lean toward the radar illumination direction in the images (the top of every image referenced in this section).

Layover is essentially an extreme case of foreshortening. As a slope facing the radar becomes steeper, echoes from its top and bottom arrive increasingly close together in time, compressing the slope in the image. When the slope becomes so steep that the local incidence angle (the angle between the radar beam and the slope's perpendicular) reaches zero, the radar beam strikes the slope directly and the entire face is recorded at once, producing a single bright, fully compressed return. If the slope becomes even steeper, the local incidence angle turns negative, meaning the top of the slope is now closer to the radar than the base. The echo from the top then arrives before the echo from the bottom, reversing their order in the image. This reversal produces the displacement effect known as layover. Since it is not possible to discriminate between the overlapping returns, there is no correction for layover.

Radar <span class="underline">shadow</span> occurs when a surface feature blocks the radar beam from reaching an area behind it, creating a region of no data return due to zero illumination. The right-most diagram in Figure [<span class="underline">8.4</span>](#figure-8.4-the-most-relevant-geometric-distortions-affecting-sar-imagery) illustrates this, as we can see that the peak of the hill completely shields the slope that descends away from the radar from receiving any illumination. The likelihood and extent of radar shadows increase with look angle, since a more side-looking geometry causes even modest elevation differences to hide parts of the terrain. Regions in shadow cannot be recovered, because the radar never received a signal from those regions. Well designed tasking is the best approach for avoiding shadow in areas of interest.

!!! note
    <span class="underline">Tasking</span> refers to the planning and selection of a satellite's imaging parameters prior to acquisition. It includes choosing the collection strategy, scene size, imaging geometry and timing parameters in a way that minimizes the negative impact of geometric distortions and ensures that the target area is imaged optimally in terms of imaging objectives.

It is important to note that dark pixels in radar imagery are not always caused by true radar shadow. Some very dark pixels are still within the radar's line of sight, but they simply exhibit low normalized radar cross section (NRCS; See Section [<span class="underline">7.4</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md#74-noise-equivalent-sigma-zero-and-the-radar-cross-section)) values. Imaging geometry can commonly render visual impressions of shadows: slopes facing away from the radar have high local incidence angles, which reduce the amount of backscattered energy and make those areas appear dark. However, low NRCS values can also result from other scattering mechanisms. For instance, in the center image depicting an urban scene in Figure [<span class="underline">8.5</span>](#figure-8.5-iceye-sar-images-illustrating-distortions), the dark pixels are likely due to specular reflection from smooth, flat surfaces such as paved streets. These surfaces reflect most of the radar energy away from the sensor rather than back toward it, producing low returns even though they are clearly visible to the radar. It is always important to consider how backscatter and geometry impact the appearance of features in a scene.

Geometric distortions relate to satellite collection geometry. In a broadside collection, layover is perpendicular to the sensor flight direction, and shadow is opposite the direction of layover (see Figure X.X, Devil's Tower). In an image oriented "shadows-down", this means tall objects lay over toward the top of the image, and shadow stretches towards the bottom. This changes in a squinted collection — because the radar is now "looking" ahead or behind broadside, the line of sight is rotated relative to the flight path. Layover will still lay towards the direction of closest approach of the flight path, but this may not be towards the top of the image. Additionally, shadow may not always be opposite the direction of layover.

This phenomenon is dictated by the slope angle, which is the angle formed between two lines: one from the ground point to the orthogonal position on the ground track, and the other from the ground point to the point of closest approach on the flight path. Slope and grazing angle are equal in a broadside collection, but differ in a squinted collection. Steeper slope angles (higher incidence angles) cause layover to increase. While "shadows-down" is generally the recommended orientation for SAR exploitation, some users may prefer "layover-up" — we leave the choice to analysts.

!!! note
    It is best practice to rotate or orient SAR images shadows-down, such that all the "shadows" (dark slopes that descend away from the radar) extend downwards (this is mentioned also in Sections [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#92-sample-arrangement) and [<span class="underline">9.3</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#93-axis-alignment)). This practice makes image interpretation more intuitive because of the way the human visual systems works, and helps a user think about the way a SAR sensor "sees" the imaged surfaces.

## 8.4) Speckle

SAR images can have a grainy, "salt and pepper appearance" - this is called <span class="underline">speckle</span>. Speckle is inherent to SAR images: It is the result of the coherent nature of the radar waves transmitted, plus the complex and varied scattering of objects at the surface. Each pixel associated with an extended target contains multiple scattering features whose returns interfere positively or negatively, resulting in a mixture of light or dark image pixels, which might be misunderstood as noise.

Speckle can be reduced using a signal processing technique called multi-looking (described in Section [<span class="underline">9.4</span>](09_data_processing_and_analysis.md#94-multi-looking-and-resolution-symmetry)). It involves dividing the synthetic aperture into several smaller, non-overlapping segments. Each segment is used to form a lower-resolution image, or "look," of the same area. Because speckle is a random and independent effect in each look, averaging them together reduces the speckle noise while preserving the underlying scene features. Multi-looking also improves the radiometric resolution and interpretability of an image, at the cost of spatial resolution. See Figure [<span class="underline">8.7</span>](#figure-8.7-multi-looking-and-speckle).

Importantly, speckle is not technically noise - it is the result of a sensor not being able to discriminate every individual scatterer on the surface. If a sensor passed over an area twice, and the sensor and the ground configuration were identical at each pass, the speckle pattern would theoretically be identical - unlike the behavior of noise. Still, speckle is commonly referred to as "speckle noise", though this is technically not accurate.

## 8.5) Motion in SAR Images

In SAR image formation, the processor uses time and relative motion to form an image, but with the assumption that the scene is static. As the sensor flies over a scene, it transmits pulses and receives echoes while moving along its flight path. Image focusing relies on the predictable Doppler history of stationary targets. For a stationary scatterer, the Doppler frequency varies smoothly and crosses zero at the point of closest approach. This is analogous to the classical Doppler effect: as an ambulance approaches, its siren sounds higher in pitch, and as it recedes, the pitch decreases.

When a target moves during acquisition, its phase history no longer matches the stationary-scene assumption used by the processor. Motion introduces additional phase components that can be interpreted as a linear term, corresponding to a Doppler frequency shift, and a quadratic term, corresponding to a Doppler rate mismatch. As a result, the moving target may appear displaced, defocused, or both.

Radial motion, meaning motion in the radar line-of-sight (range direction), introduces an additional Doppler frequency shift that causes azimuth displacement. If the target velocity is sufficiently large, the associated quadratic phase mismatch also produces azimuth defocusing, and residual range migration may cause range defocusing. Radial motion can therefore result in both displacement and loss of focus.

Motion primarily along the flight direction (azimuth direction) does not significantly shift the Doppler centroid in typical broadside geometries, but it does alter the Doppler rate by adding an extra quadratic phase term over the aperture. This rate mismatch relative to the stationary scene leads to azimuth defocusing without a systematic azimuth displacement.

In practice, moving objects often have both radial and along-track velocity components, resulting in a combination of azimuth displacement, azimuth defocusing, and possible range migration effects. These signatures are not anomalies or ambiguities, but well understood and expected consequences of the static-scene assumption. Importantly, they can be exploited to detect moving targets and to estimate aspects of their motion during SAR acquisition.

## 8.6) Image Quality Metrics

The quality of a SAR image extends beyond the spatial resolution and geospatial accuracy metrics described in Chapter [<span class="underline">6</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md), as well as the noise performance and radiometric resolution metrics presented in Chapter [<span class="underline">7</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md). SAR image quality is ultimately defined by how interpretable and informative the image is for its intended use. Two key metrics are commonly used to characterize SAR image quality: the Radar National Imagery Interpretability Rating Scale (RNIIRS) and the Radar General Image Quality Equation (RGIQE). These metrics approach the concept of quality from different perspectives — subjective interpretability versus objective information content.

The <span class="underline">Radar National Imagery Interpretability Rating Scale (RNIIRS or Radar NIIRS)</span> adapts the established NIIRS framework for radar imagery. It provides a subjective assessment of an image's interpretability based on the tasks it enables an experienced analyst to perform. The scale ranges from 0, indicating an image too degraded to support interpretation, to 10, representing imagery of exceptional clarity and utility. While widely used in military and intelligence contexts, RNIIRS depends on trained human evaluation and scenario-specific criteria, making it less useful for consistent, system-level comparisons across sensors or acquisition modes.

For example, in a certain RNIIRS reference, images with ratings of 4/10 and 7/10 should allow the analyst to "distinguish between large rotary-wing and medium fixed-wing aircraft" and "identify small fighter aircraft by type", respectively, which are useful criteria only to a very specific niche of users and use cases. Therefore, only specialists would be able to rate images in the RNIIRS scale and separate concrete reference definitions are required in each field to help the different specialists in the field to keep their scales uniform.

In contrast, the <span class="underline">Radar General Image Quality Equation (RGIQE or Radar GIQE)</span> offers a physics-based, quantitative measure of SAR image quality grounded in information theory. Adapted from the GIQE developed by the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA), the RGIQE draws on the Shannon-Hartley theorem, which defines the theoretical maximum information capacity of a communication channel:

\[C = B\log_{2}\left( 1 + \frac{S}{N} \right\]

Here, \(C\) is the information throughput, \(B\) is the system bandwidth, and \(\frac{S}{N}\) is the signal-to-noise ratio. Applying this to SAR, each image resolution cell can be treated as a channel the information content of which depends on the system's bandwidth and the level of noise affecting the image. Because SAR resolution is governed by both range bandwidth and azimuth bandwidth, the maximum information content per unit area can be expressed as:

\[I = B_{\text{az}}B_{R_{\text{ground}}}\log_{2}\left( 1 + \frac{S}{N} \right\]

where \(I\) represents information density in bits per square meter and \(B\) is replaced with terms for both azimuth and ground range bandwidth. This formulation links directly to fundamental SAR parameters and captures the degrading effects of thermal noise, quantization noise, sidelobes, and ambiguities, some of which were discussed in Chapters [<span class="underline">6</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md) and [<span class="underline">7</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md).

RGIQE balances two fundamental pillars: bandwidth (which determines spatial resolution) and signal-to-noise ratio (commonly represented by NESZ, introduced in Section [<span class="underline">7.4</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md#74-noise-equivalent-sigma-zero-and-the-radar-cross-section)). These pillars cannot be increased simultaneously because thermal noise power grows linearly with bandwidth (Section [<span class="underline">7.3</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md#73-the-challenge-of-noise-and-the-power-of-processing-gains)), creating an inherent trade-off. Systems must therefore optimize between finer resolution and better noise performance depending on their application requirements.

Despite this trade-off, or actually exactly because it acknowledges it, RGIQE remains a powerful quality metric. A higher value of \(I\) suggests the SAR system delivers more potentially usable information per pixel, making it more informative than bandwidth, spatial resolution, or NESZ considered in isolation.

Equating information *content* with image *quality* must be approached critically, however. The notion of bits per square meter as a figure of merit assumes that all bits are equally useful and equally accessible to analysts or downstream algorithms. In practice, information content alone does not capture semantic clarity, feature contrast, or contextual interpretability. A SAR image with high theoretical information density may still be operationally limited by artefacts, clutter, calibration issues, poor choice of imaging geometry, or even the nature of the observed scene.

The dual use of RNIIRS and RGIQE reflects this tension: one metric is tuned to subjective human judgment and operational relevance, the other to theoretical computational capacity. For SAR product developers and system designers, it is therefore essential to consider both when assessing and optimizing system performance, while remaining aware of their respective limitations. !!! note
    In this chapter, we introduced the dual nature of SAR data. Both phase and amplitude are measured for every pixel, which can be leveraged to reveal information about the scene. We then highlighted many concepts to take under consideration when interpreting a radar image. What was the imaging geometry during acquisition, and how are objects in an image oriented relative to the sensor? What is the polarization of the sensor? What are the material properties and moisture content of the objects in the image, and how did those impact their reflectivity? What geometric distortions and scattering mechanisms are present in this image? Has this image been multilooked to reduce speckle? If an image is technically imperfect, but contains mission critical information, how do we ultimately determine its quality? Learning these fundamentals enables a more complete understanding of why radar images look the way they do, and allows users to understand the breadth and depth of information contained in a SAR image.
