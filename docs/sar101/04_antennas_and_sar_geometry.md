# 4\) Antennas and SAR Geometry

!!! summary inline end

    - Active Phased Array Antennas (APAAs) consist of thousands of antenna elements which leverage the principle of interference to steer the antenna beam at lightning speed and without physical movement.

    - Beamwidth is inversely proportional to aperture size and thus larger antennas create narrower, more focused beams. However, aperture synthesis reduces the importance of large physical antennas.

    - The look angle, depression angle, incidence angle, and grazing angle describe the geometric relationship between the sensor, beam, and target surface.

    - Local surface variations affect angle measurements: True incidence and grazing angles vary across scenes due to topography, requiring consideration of local surface characteristics rather than just nominal values.

    - Integration angle determines synthetic aperture effectiveness: the subtended angle during data collection (typically ~8° for a 10-second illumination) determines the length of the synthetic aperture and thus the amount of aperture synthesis that can be achieved.

    - While most SAR imaging is broadside to the flight direction, squinting the beam forwards and backwards during collection allows flexibility in targeting and coverage.

We introduced basic concepts and terms in Chapter [<span class="underline">1</span>](01_introduction.md), including the definitions for aperture, beamwidth, illumination, scatterer and backscatter. In this chapter, we will dive deeper into the concepts of radar antennas and SAR geometry.

## 4.1) Active Phased Array Antennas, Boresight and Gain

In a radar system, the antenna serves a dual purpose: it transmits electromagnetic (EM) waves toward a target and receives the backscattered energy reflected from objects in the scene. At a physical level, this process involves the motion of free electrons within the antenna structure. During transmission, these electrons are driven into oscillation by an applied alternating current. According to the laws of physics, accelerating electric charges emit electromagnetic radiation, which is how the radar signal is launched into space. On reception, incoming EM waves cause the electrons in the antenna to oscillate once again. This is similar to how ripples on a lake cause a floating buoy to bob up and down—the incoming wave "shakes" the free electrons in the antenna, allowing the radar system to detect and measure the returning signal.

<span class="underline">Antenna gain</span> describes how well an antenna focuses energy in a particular direction. You can think of it by comparing a flashlight to a bare lightbulb: a lightbulb shines in all directions, but a flashlight concentrates the same amount of energy into a narrow beam, making it brighter in that direction. Similarly, an antenna with higher gain doesn’t create more power—it just focuses the energy more efficiently in a specific direction, making the signal stronger where it is aimed. This helps the radar see farther or more clearly in that direction.

<span class="underline">Antenna boresight</span> refers to the axis of maximum gain (for both radiated and received power) of a directional antenna, such as the common satellite dish, which is a type of parabolic antenna. The boresight usually aligns with the antenna’s physical axis of symmetry. However, if the boresight axis can be adjusted, then the <span class="underline">boresight angle</span> is the angle between the boresight axis and the physical axis of symmetry.

Radar systems typically use one of two types of antennas: <span class="underline">reflector antennas</span> or <span class="underline">phased arrays</span>.

A <span class="underline">reflector antenna</span> works much like an optical mirror. On transmission, a smaller feed antenna generates the electromagnetic waves, which are then focused and directed by a large curved surface—similar to how a satellite dish or flashlight reflector concentrates light. On reception, the process works in reverse: the reflector collects incoming waves and focuses them onto the feed, much like a telescope mirror focusing starlight onto a sensor.

A <span class="underline">Phased Array Antenna (PAA)</span>, on the other hand, is made up of many small individual antenna elements arranged in a regular pattern. By precisely controlling the timing (or phase) of the signal at each element, the array can electronically steer the direction of the beam without moving the antenna itself. This makes phased arrays highly flexible and fast, allowing radar systems to rapidly scan large areas or track multiple targets. ICEYE SAR satellites are equipped with PAAs.

The operation of phased array antennas is illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">4.1</span>](#figure-4.1-phased-array-antenna-beam-steering). In a phased array, each individual antenna element (denoted with A in the figure) emits its own spherical wavefront with the electric power from the transmitter (TX). When all the elements transmit together, the resulting radiation pattern is formed by summing their individual wavefronts. The superposition principle leads to the phenomenon of constructive and destructive <span class="underline">interference</span>, where waves from different elements reinforce each other in some directions and cancel in others, shaping the overall radiation pattern into a <span class="underline">plane wave</span>. If all the elements emit in sync, the waves add up most strongly directly in front of the array. However, by introducing a computer (C) that controls phase shifters (\(\phi\)) to induce small phase shifts (i.e., delays) in the signals sent to each element, the combined wavefront can be tilted. This tilting steers the beam in a desired direction (boresight angle \(\theta\) in the figure), without physically moving the antenna. Note that most phased arrays have two-dimensional arrays of antennas instead of the linear array shown in Figure [<span class="underline">4.1</span>](#figure-4.1-phased-array-antenna-beam-steering), and then the beam can be steered in two dimensions.

On reception, the same principle applies in reverse. An incoming wavefront arrives at each antenna element at slightly different times depending on its direction of arrival. This creates phase differences between the signals received by each element. By analyzing these differences, the system can determine the direction the signal came from, or selectively "listen" in a specific direction by adding the signals in a way that reinforces waves from that angle.

## 4.2) Beamwidth and Illumination Power

In this section we expand on the concepts of beamwidth and illumination that were introduced in Section [<span class="underline">1.1</span>](01_introduction.md#11-radar-imaging-antenna-aperture-and-beamwidth).

When an antenna transmits or receives energy, it forms a <span class="underline">beam pattern</span>—a shape that shows how strongly the antenna radiates or receives energy in different directions. This pattern results from interference between waves coming from different parts of the antenna. Whether it is a phased array with many small elements, or a reflector antenna with a curved surface, each part of the antenna contributes waves that combine together. Where these waves align in phase, they add up (constructive interference) to form strong signals, and where they are out of phase, they cancel out (destructive interference), creating nulls or weaker spots. This is why beam patterns have a strong central peak (also called <span class="underline">mainlobe</span>) and weaker <span class="underline">sidelobes</span> around it. See Figure [<span class="underline">4.2</span>](#figure-4.2-antenna-propagation-patterns) for illustrations of beam patterns for a simple isotropic antenna element, and three array antennas.

A common way to describe the width of this mainlobe or beam is the Half Power Beamwidth (HPBW). This is the angular width of the beam between the two directions where the signal strength falls to half of its maximum value. It gives a good sense of how "focused" the antenna is. A narrower HPBW means the antenna is more directional and can better separate targets that are close together in angle. Figure [<span class="underline">4.3</span>](#figure-4.3-antenna-beamwidths-and-illumination-power) illustrates the HPBW and related terms.

</figure>

As mentioned earlier, the beamwidth of a radar antenna is inversely proportional to the aperture size. Mathematically, this can be expressed using the formula

\[\theta = \frac{\lambda}{D}\]

Here, \(\lambda\) is the wavelength of the electromagnetic waves, \(D\) is the physical aperture or antenna size in the plane of interest (of which there are two for rectangular antennas). As shown by the equation, the smaller the wavelength, the larger the antenna and aperture, the narrower the radar beam, and thus the sharper and more intense the illumination. We can see how microwaves combined with large apertures can enable high quality radar detection, ranging and imaging. Additionally, higher illumination intensity improves the <span class="underline">Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)</span>, which describes the strength and clarity of the desired signal in the backscatter. The SNR is highest from the scatterers closest to the beam’s maximum power area, while scatterers closer to the zero power beamwidths can have such poor SNR that they are barely represented in the image.

As an example, most ICEYE satellites operate in the X-band, with a wavelength of approximately 3.1 cm. The antennas of generation 2,3, and 3.5 satellites measure 3.2 meters in the horizontal (azimuth) direction and 0.4 meters in the vertical (elevation) direction, which results in Half Power Beamwidths (HPBW) of about 0.49° in azimuth and 3.95° in elevation. These beamwidths determine how wide the radar beam is in each direction. At a typical orbit height of around 550 kilometers, this corresponds to a radar footprint—the area illuminated on the ground—of roughly 6 kilometers in the azimuth direction and 35 kilometers in range. This defines the swath of Earth's surface that is actively illuminated by the radar signal.

In SAR, the size of the illuminated footprint on the ground (determined by the beamwidth of the antenna) is not directly related to image resolution. In fact, SAR uses special processing techniques which we will explore in later sections to achieve much finer resolution than what the physical beamwidth alone would allow. In the azimuth direction, for example, SAR synthesizes a large virtual aperture by combining signals from multiple pulses over time, effectively bypassing the limitations of beamwidth. In the range direction, a broader beam is often beneficial, as it illuminates a wider swath of the Earth's surface which allows larger areas to be imaged during each orbit. Despite aperture synthesis, returns from the edges of the entire collection footprint have low SNR and are very faint, and are often cropped out of the final image to ensure image quality.

## 4.3) Imaging Geometry and Related Angles

Now that we understand the basics of how SAR antennas and beams can be directed and steered, let us proceed onto the terms used to describe radar illumination and imaging geometry. Essential angles and terms are illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">4.4</span>](#figure-4.4-radar-imaging-angles).

As described in Section [<span class="underline">1.2</span>](01_introduction.md#12-side-looking-illumination-and-ranging), most radar imaging is done broadside —that is, perpendicular to the flight direction. However, some systems can collect data in an off-broadside or squinted orientation. The <span class="underline">squint angle</span> refers to the angle between the radar’s line of sight and the broadside direction (see Figure [<span class="underline">4.4</span>](#figure-4.4-radar-imaging-angles)). Intuitively, squinting means the radar beam is aimed slightly forward or backward along the flight path, rather than directly to the side. In the context of SAR, the squint angle is typically defined at a single point in time along the flight trajectory, usually at the center of the synthetic aperture. This provides a consistent reference for describing the viewing geometry, even though the radar's line of sight can vary throughout the aperture (depending on the illumination mode; See Chapter [<span class="underline">5</span>](05_collection_strategies.md)). The <span class="underline">ground track</span> is the path the satellite follows as it orbits the Earth. <span class="underline">Footprint</span>, or scene, refers to the surface area over which data is collected.

<span class="underline">Look angle</span> is the angle between the satellite’s nadir and the line-of-sight vector. <span class="underline">Depression angle</span> is the angle between the radar beam and the local level of the sensor, and is the complement of the look angle. Conversely, <span class="underline">incidence angle</span> is the angle between the line-of-sight and the <span class="underline">local vertical</span>. If the ground is flat, then this vertical is equal to the <span class="underline">zenith</span>, the direction opposite of the nadir. The complement of the incidence angle is the <span class="underline">grazing angle</span> which is the angle between the line of sight and the tangent to the local ground surface at the point of incidence.

Understanding these angles is important because they directly affect how SAR images are acquired and interpreted. In spaceborne SAR, the satellite’s orbit height is fixed, so changing the incidence angle means pointing the radar to illuminate areas either closer or farther away in range. This has two major consequences. First, because radar systems have limited transmitted power, increasing the incidence angle too much means the radar signal must travel farther, and less of it returns to the sensor. This reduces the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and makes it harder to detect weaker targets. Second, the incidence angle also influences how well the radar can resolve features on the ground. Since SAR images are formed in range (distance from the sensor), and the radar is looking to the side, the incidence angle determines how this range resolution projects onto the ground. At very low incidence angles, we would struggle to separate targets in range, effectively losing resolution. At higher incidence angles, the projected ground resolution improves and becomes closer to the slant range resolution. So, there is a trade-off: larger incidence angles improve ground resolution but weaken the return signal, while smaller angles provide stronger returns but degrade spatial resolution. These concepts are revisited in Chapter [<span class="underline">6</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md) and Chapter [<span class="underline">7</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md).

Illustrations such as Figure [<span class="underline">4.4</span>](#figure-4.4-radar-imaging-angles) are simplified in order to highlight geometric relationships, but do not capture the complexities of Earth’s surface. The true incidence and grazing angles can vary in a scene due to <span class="underline">topography</span>: uneven terrain, vegetation, urban infrastructure and other natural and artificial features can impact the angle at which a radar beam interacts with the surface. The terms <span class="underline">local incidence angle</span> and <span class="underline">local grazing angle</span> are used when referring to the geometric relationship a radar beam has with a local surface.

It should be noted that all the angles, levels, and verticals vary between different pulses and different parts of a scene, especially across larger footprints. Most numerical values offered for these variables therefore normally represent values measured at the mid-aperture point of the SAR collection.

This brings us to the final angle that relates to the very essence of SAR. The term <span class="underline">integration angle</span> describes the angular extent over which the radar observes a specific target as the satellite moves along its orbit. In other words, it's the angle between the target and the satellite’s starting and ending positions during the time the target is being illuminated (see Figure [<span class="underline">4.5</span>](#figure-4.5-integration-angle)). The larger this angle, the more echoed wave measurements are collected, and the better the achievable resolution in the azimuth (along-track) direction. A 25 second illumination and imaging period of a satellite in LEO (see Chapter [<span class="underline">2</span>](02_satellite_orbits_and_constellations.md)) can result in an orbit segment of about 200 km, generating an integration angle of about 20°.
