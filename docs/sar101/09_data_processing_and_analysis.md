# 9\) Data Processing and Analysis

!!! summary inline end

    - SAR data processing transforms raw radar measurements into a hierarchy of data levels (0–2), ranging from unfocused echo data to fully processed, analysis-ready imagery and derived products.

    - Level 0 data (raw and compensated phase history data) records unfocused echoes and essential calibration/orbit metadata, supporting advanced signal-domain workflows but rarely used directly by most end users.

    - Level 1 products such as Single Look Complex (SLC) images are formed by focusing phase history data in range and azimuth, applying motion compensation and radiometric calibration to produce coherent, geometrically accurate complex images.

    - Level 1 data can be further processed into ground-projected amplitude products like Ground Range Detected (GRD) images through detection, multi-looking, and reprojection, trading phase information and some resolution for interpretability and ease of use.

    - Level 2 derivative products (e.g., interferograms, coherence maps, change detection layers, and advanced motion or deformation products) are generated by applying specialized analysis techniques to level 0 and 1 inputs.

    - Throughout the processing chain, metadata and container formats play a crucial role in preserving acquisition parameters, calibration coefficients, and geometric context so that downstream users can correctly interpret and exploit the data.

To provide useful data for consumers of SAR data and automated analytical pipelines, a series of steps are performed to transform collected SAR imaging data into various data products. This chapter provides an overview of the data processing flow and explains some of the most basic processing options, and describes several SAR data analysis techniques and resulting derivative products. While some data products are mentioned here, Section [<span class="underline">10</span>](10_data_products.md) presents them and their use cases in more detail.

## 9.1) Data Processing Flow and Resulting Products

All SAR data and data products can be classified and grouped into data processing levels. A <span class="underline">data processing level</span> describes the amount of processing that the data has been subjected to, ranging from 0 (least processed) to 2 (most processed).

SAR data products at each level include and require metadata. <span class="underline">Metadata</span> refers to the information that provides context for the rest of the data. SAR metadata includes descriptive information such as acquisition time, sensor settings and geometry, and georeferencing which is crucial for the proper interpretation and utilization of the data. Metadata also includes information about the structure of the data and metadata and their organization in the container file (See Article [<span class="underline">8</span>](../techdocs/08_sar_data_container_file_and_metadata_formats.md) in the Technical Documentation for a discussion on container file formats). The processing steps performed on the data as well as the associated choices and parameters are also recorded in the metadata.

As we have discussed, SAR image generation begins with the emission of thousands of coherent pulses and the subsequent measurement and decomposition of each backscatter echo into raw measures of time, amplitude and phase. After the imaging operation has taken place, the recorded unfocused <span class="underline">raw radar measurements</span> (or echo data) are compressed and downloaded – along with metadata relating to calibration and orbit information, and acquisition time and date – from the SAR satellite into one of the terminals in the SAR operator’s ground station network. Raw radar measurements represent <span class="underline">level 0 data</span> and are rarely used without further processing, except by SAR system developers and researchers in the field of signal processing.

The first part of the subsequent processing flow is called <span class="underline">Phase History Data (PHD) processing</span> because it accounts for the changes over time of the phase values of each scatterer. Additional compensations, such as those for hardware timing and platform motion, and corrections for propagation effects, such as those from atmospheric distortion, are incorporated. The data is corrected for instrumental and geometric distortions but it is neither calibrated nor geolocated. Sensor specific calibration constants and satellite specific orbit information are included in the metadata along with descriptions of the processing performed. At this stage the data and metadata could be packaged and productized as the least processed data product commonly called <span class="underline">Compensated Phase History Data (CPHD)</span>, presented in Section [<span class="underline">10.1</span>](10_data_products.md#101-compensated-phase-history-data-cphd). Because it still offers signal data that is yet to be transformed into an image space, it is considered to be level 0 data.

To get to <span class="underline">level 1 data products</span> the phase history data is <span class="underline">focused</span> with an appropriate algorithm into the azimuth and range components of each resolution cell to produce a two dimensional complex-valued image. Focusing is also called compression and is done separately for range and azimuth. <span class="underline">Range compression</span> involves correlating the backscatter data with the transmitted chirps while <span class="underline">azimuth compression</span> involves combining the radar echoes from multiple pulses along the flight path. The focusing algorithm used depends on the chosen collection strategy. This part of image formation is often the most computationally intensive phase in SAR data processing, as it involves applying convolution or Fourier transforms to vast amounts of signal data, as well as time-domain processing, interpolation and integration.

The focused radar returns get aligned in the slant range coordinate system, corresponding to the direct sensor-to-target line of sight, and more refined compensation is applied to ensure that the mapping of the data across the focused image is consistent, coherent and has high geometric accuracy. This includes full <span class="underline">motion compensation</span> where any deviations in the satellite’s nominal trajectory caused by orbital dynamics or altitude variations are eliminated to prevent phase errors and to maintain geometric fidelity in the final image. While basic motion compensation is performed in CPHD production, this additional step is more comprehensive.

<span class="underline">Radiometric calibration</span> coefficients are typically computed at this stage to enable the conversion of focused pixel amplitudes into standardized backscatter measures such as sigma nought (NRCS defined in Section [<span class="underline">7.4</span>](07_separating_signals_from_noise.md#74-noise-equivalent-sigma-zero-and-the-radar-cross-section)). Spatially varying effects, including range-dependent attenuation, antenna gain variation, and geometric spreading loss, are compensated within the processor on a pixel-by-pixel basis. In addition to these corrections, a radiometric calibration constant is provided in the metadata. This calibration factor is a scalar applied uniformly across the image to scale focused pixel values into physically meaningful backscatter units. ICEYE provides the necessary calibration factors in the metadata but leaves the actual conversion to the user.

At this point in processing, we get to the level 1 data products. The <span class="underline">Single-Look Complex (SLC)</span>, presented in Section [<span class="underline">10.2</span>](10_data_products.md#102-single-look-complex-slc-images), is the most widely used product for advanced workflows leveraging SAR phase data.

The <span class="underline">Ground Range Detected (GRD)</span>, described in Section [<span class="underline">10.4</span>](10_data_products.md#104-ground-range-detected-grd-images), has been detected and projected to the ground plane (see Section [<span class="underline">9.4</span>](#94-projection-surfaces)). Depending on the imaging mode, the GRD may also be multi-looked (see Section [<span class="underline">9.3</span>](#93-multi-looking-and-resolution-symmetry)). The GRD includes the amplitude data, but phase-offset data is discarded to minimize file size. This means the GRD cannot be used for applications that rely on phase data, such as Coherent Change Detection (described in Section 9.7). The GRD is a convenient format for many SAR applications with no or minimal additional processing.

The <span class="underline">Sensor Independent Complex Data (SICD)</span>, discussed in Section [<span class="underline">10.3</span>](10_data_products.md#103-sensor-independent-complex-and-derived-data-sicd-sidd), has essentially the same information as an SLC, but additional processing and specific packaging are applied to minimize differences between SICD products from different SAR sensors, platforms and vendors. Both SICD and SLC products include the full complex data, but they are not interchangeable. <span class="underline">Sensor Independent Derived Data (</span>SIDD) products, which are often grouped with the SICD, involve additional processing to generate application-ready imagery and are thus level 2 data products. SIDDs have been multilooked and projected to the ground plane. SICD image data is in the same format regardless of processing algorithm or collection strategy. In theory, any algorithm that uses SICD will work on SAR imagery from any SAR vendor. Both the SICD and the SIDD are stored in the NITF (National Imagery Transmission Format) container. For this reason, these files can only be used with GIS viewers and algorithms that know how to handle this type of dataset.

The GRD, SIDD, SLC, and SICD are all stored as binary data in a special container file format that facilitates further processing and exploitation (see Article [<span class="underline">8</span>](../techdocs/08_sar_data_container_file_and_metadata_formats.md) in the Technical Documentation).

## 9.2) Sample Arrangement

<span class="underline">Sample arrangement</span> determines the organization of image data in the container file. Like most digital images, SAR images are also stored as a <span class="underline">raster</span>. SAR data is collected in the range-azimuth geometry and has two natural arrangements: shadows-down and azimuth-down.

In the <span class="underline">shadows-down arrangement</span> each row of pixels represents a narrow portion of the scene in the azimuth direction (along the satellite’s flight track), and each column a portion in the range direction (across-track) from top (near range) to bottom (far range). Visually, one can imagine a satellite orbiting while illuminating a scene adjacent to its flight path. The top of the image is closest to the satellite, and energy is projected down range, resulting in shadows ‘pointing down’ in the image. This orientation is generally recommended for visual SAR interpretation.

The <span class="underline">azimuth-down arrangement</span> is an alternative where the azimuth dimension is rendered vertically following the downward motion of the SAR satellite either left or right of the scene, and the shadows project horizontally to the other side. When the satellite is on the left, the shadows project rightward and the more unambiguous term would be the <span class="underline">shadows-right azimuth-down arrangement</span>. This arrangement is referred to as the <span class="underline">native arrangement</span> or data orientation and is illustrated by the matrix at the center and the imaging geometry sketch on the left side of Figure [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](#figure-9.2-a-shadows-right-azimuth-down-sample-arrangement). The right side illustrates the other variant, namely the <span class="underline">shadows-left azimuth-down arrangement</span>.

Both the shadows-down and azimuth-down arrangements can be converted to each other with simple 90° rotations and flips, making the transformation between them quick and lossless. For example, by rotating the raster depicted at the center top of Figure [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](#figure-9.2-a-shadows-right-azimuth-down-sample-arrangement) by 90° clockwise we get to the shadows-down azimuth-left arrangement.

Samples can also be arranged in other orientations, most commonly to align the data with a map coordinate system. <span class="underline">Map-oriented SAR images</span> are often larger than the original range–azimuth images because reorienting and projecting the data onto a map grid requires geometric rotation and resampling. When the rotated image is placed back into a rectangular raster, the bounding box expands and blank (no-data) pixels must be added to fill the “no-data triangles” at the sides of the rotated footprint. If the map is in standard orientation such that each pixel column is aligned with north at the top of the image, then this can also be called the <span class="underline">north-up arrangement</span>.

While the north-up and other arrangements may be preferred in some contexts, the azimuth and shadows-down arrangements are generally recommended for single-scene radar imagery viewing and exploitation. These orientations are directly based on the range-azimuth acquisition geometry and fully preserve the geometric fidelity of the data. In contrast, all arrangements that require non-square-angle rotations involve resampling and interpolation, which can introduce resampling artifacts, such as blurring or pixelation, which decrease image sharpness and quality. In addition to their file size and image quality advantages, the azimuth- and shadows-down arrangements also simplify interpretation, as the geometric distortions that are inherent to all SAR imagery are easy to detect and interpret.

Another reason shadows- and azimuth-down arrangements are often preferred for individual scenes is that most image analysts and GIS software tools can apply rotations and map projections dynamically at the point of use, which is also why the axis alignment option explained in Section [<span class="underline">9.3</span>](#93-axis-alignment) is useful. Thus, unless the use case is precisely known in advance, retaining a technically natural sampling arrangement is often the wisest choice.

Finally, it must be noted that the azimuth-down arrangement allows a collection to be efficiently extended by concatenating more data to the end of the raster, which can be valuable in large-scale mapping. This is the technical reason for it being chosen as the native arrangement in SAR.

## 9.3) Axis Alignment

Unlike sample arrangement, <span class="underline">axis alignment</span> does not impact how the data is stored in the file. Instead, it guides image processing and viewing software to orient and display the scene in a specific way without changing the sample arrangement. Technically, the sample arrangement already provides a hard-written axis alignment, but this processing option allows the designation of a recommendation or default that can supersede the hard-written one.

The axis alignment options are the same as for sample arrangement (See Section [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](#92-sample-arrangement)) but since data storage is not impacted, this option can be reverted later. Modern GIS software allows users to select how to orient SAR data, whether by native sampling, map alignment, or some other display preference.

## 9.4) Multi-Looking and Resolution Symmetry

<span class="underline">Multi-looking</span> refers to the process of computing the spatial average of adjacent SAR image pixels. As explained in Section [<span class="underline">6.4</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md#64-spatial-resolution-and-impulse-response), resolution in the azimuth and range are determined by independent factors. Therefore, the resulting resolutions are quite different in magnitude: azimuth resolution is usually higher than range resolution. This difference causes pixels to be elongated in data products that have not been multilooked, like the SLC. Multilooking can be applied to create square pixels, which are easier for visual interpretation. This averaging, through a process of incoherent summing, reduces speckle and improves radiometric resolution due to increased amplitude reliability for each pixel at the cost of spatial resolution. Figure [<span class="underline">8.7</span>](08_sar_imagery_and_interpretation_considerations.md#figure-8.7-multi-looking-and-speckle) illustrates multi-looking within Section [<span class="underline">8.4</span>](08_sar_imagery_and_interpretation_considerations.md#84-speckle) in conjunction with speckle.

Multi-looking in the azimuth direction is performed by dividing the synthetic aperture into smaller sub-apertures, each representing a subset of the full synthetic aperture, and then combining them. Multi-looking in the range direction involves dividing the range data into frequency sub-bands or simply averaging neighbouring range pixels. Sometimes multi-looking is done in both directions, although multi-looking in range is not common in SAR.

The individual sub-apertures (or frequency sub-bands when multi-looking in range) are called <span class="underline">looks</span>. The terms <span class="underline">multi-look</span> and <span class="underline">single-look</span> are used to emphasize whether multi-looking has been applied or not. A single-look image has the original full resolution while multi-look images usually provide symmetric resolution, reduced speckle and improved radiometric resolution. Additionally, multi-looked images often consume less memory, as pixel counts may be reduced to a fraction at least in one dimension. ICEYE amplitude image data products are multi-looked, with the number of looks changing with respect to imaging mode.

## 9.5) Projection Surfaces

As discussed in Chapter [<span class="underline">1.1</span>](01_introduction.md#11-radar-imaging-antenna-aperture-and-beamwidth), SAR data is collected in the slant range. The sensor measures the line-of-sight distance from the satellite to the surface, which is different from measuring that distance on the ground. In order to analyze data, we need to transform the data by projecting it to a ground surface, or <span class="underline">projection surface</span>.

The projection surface determines how SAR pixel data from the raster is geometrically mapped when the image is displayed, analyzed, or integrated with other geospatial data. The choice of projection surface is a fundamental processing decision that affects both the geometric fidelity of the imagery and its suitability for different applications.

The <span class="underline">slant plane</span> refers to the radar's native <span class="underline">slant range geometry</span>, where pixels are arranged according to the direct sensor-to-target line of sight and azimuth position along the flight path. This is used by default for SLC and SICD data products as it is the natural coordinate system for focused SAR data and introduces no additional geometric transformations. The slant plane preserves the highest geometric consistency with the raw measurements and avoids artifacts that can arise from reprojection.

The <span class="underline">ground plane</span> refers to the <span class="underline">ground range geometry</span>, where pixels are arranged on a flat horizontal tangential surface at a specified elevation, with ground range and azimuth as the two dimensions. It is appropriate for flat terrain over limited spatial extents, such as agricultural fields or coastal areas, and makes imagery more map-like and easier to interpret, but does not account for terrain relief.

Note that whenever a projection surface other than the slant plane is chosen, multilooking is commonly applied to achieve pixel resolution symmetry in the desired projection, as described in Section <span class="underline">9.4</span>.

An <span class="underline">ellipsoid surface</span> or an <span class="underline">Earth Ellipsoid Model (EEM)</span> is a mathematical representation of the general shape of the Earth’s surface, which takes into account the elliptic shape of the Earth (wider at the equator and flattened at the poles). In this case, each pixel is assigned to the point where the radar line of sight intersects the ellipsoid, ensuring a consistent geometric framework across the full scene extent. Because the full ellipsoidal curvature is applied, even for large scenes spanning hundreds of kilometres, the result is a globally coherent representation that avoids distortions that would arise from projecting onto a flat local plane. However, as terrain relief is not considered, flipped terrain-perception effects can arise in which mountains appear to be valleys and valleys appear elevated. Indeed, pixel positions simply correspond to where the terrain would lie if the Earth surface followed the ellipsoid exactly. The <span class="underline">World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84)</span> is the most commonly used global reference ellipsoid for geodetically referenced SAR imagery. This is the standard choice for amplitude image data products such as the GRD. Coarse georeferencing is often done for images projected on an EEM.

A <span class="underline">topographic surface</span> or <span class="underline">Digital Elevation Model (DEM)</span> is a numerical representation of the Earth’s surface that contains actual and interpolated elevation points (altitude or elevation above sea level) describing bare-earth topography. As it accounts for terrain relief, some geometric distortions can be minimized (see foreshortening in Section [<span class="underline">8.3</span>](08_sar_imagery_and_interpretation_considerations.md#83-geometric-distortions)) and some misinterpretations avoided. Indeed, using a DEM can significantly improve geospatial accuracy and image interpretability in areas with variable topography. As described in Section [<span class="underline">6.5</span>](06_spatial_resolution_and_geospatial_accuracy.md#65-geolocation-geospatial-accuracy-and-orthorectification), accurate orthorectification requires a high quality DEM. Note that a DEM always has a specific EEM as its geodetic reference frame, with which it can provide latitude and longitude coordinates for any of its points, for instance.

<span class="underline">Digital Surface Models (DSMs)</span> are sometimes considered a sub-category of DEMs. However, they go clearly above regular DEMs,as they account for above-ground features such as vegetation canopy and buildings, and not just the bare-earth topography. Since X-band SAR pulses do not effectively penetrate buildings nor dense vegetation, high resolution DSMs provide the most accurate projection surface model for what the SAR pixels actually represent. Actually, DSMs themselves are often constructed with data from SAR interferometry (See Section <span class="underline">9.7</span>) and/or other remote sensing techniques.

Figure [<span class="underline">9.3</span>](#figure-9.3-earth-ellipsoid-digital-elevation-and-digital-surface-models-illustrated) illustrates the differences between EEM, DEM and DSM as projection surfaces.

## 9.6) Amplitude-Based Analysis Techniques

While sections [<span class="underline">9.2</span>](#92-sample-arrangement) through [<span class="underline">9.5</span>](#95-projection-surfaces) focused on standard image preparation options, this and subsequent sections focus on data analysis: They list several techniques with which SAR data can be converted into descriptive level 2 data products, and ultimately into valuable insight.

<span class="underline">Amplitude-based techniques</span> extract information from radar backscatter intensity without requiring phase coherence between acquisitions. Therefore, the GRD and SIDD data products, described in Sections [<span class="underline">10.3</span>](10_data_products.md#103-sensor-independent-complex-and-derived-data-sicd-sidd) and [<span class="underline">10.4</span>](#ground-range-detected-grd-images), are ideal input for these techniques. The amplitude-based techniques described below are robust across acquisition geometries and applicable to all collection modes, making them widely used in operational settings. Notable amplitude data analysis techniques include the following:

<span class="underline">Backscatter mosaics</span>, or <span class="underline">Analysis-Ready Data (ARD) layers</span>, are standardized composites of radiometrically normalized and geolocated amplitude images aligned on a common grid. By fusing scenes from different times, angles, or tracks, they provide a consistent surface-reflectivity baseline. ARD mosaics reduce variability between acquisitions and support applications such as land-cover mapping, environmental monitoring, long-term trend analysis, and machine-learning workflows by shifting effort from preprocessing to interpretation.

<span class="underline">Classification and Detection</span> methods segment images into meaningful categories. Thresholding applies fixed or adaptive cutoffs to separate classes such as water and land. Unsupervised classification (K-means, ISODATA) clusters similar pixels without training data, while supervised approaches (SVMs, Random Forests, Neural Networks) use labeled examples. Texture-based classification integrates spatial descriptors like entropy and contrast, and Object-Based Image Analysis (OBIA) groups pixels into regions for context-aware classification. Anomaly detection highlights unexpected patterns, and object detection localizes targets such as ships or buildings through template matching or deep learning. Figure [<span class="underline">9.4</span>](#figure-9.4-an-illustration-of-land-cover-mapping) illustrates a land cover classification map where each pixel is designated to represent either bare soil, cropland, forest, water or built-up area.

<span class="underline">Amplitude Change Detection (ACD)</span> identifies variations in backscatter between co-registered and radiometrically equalized images. Difference or ratio metrics highlight changes, which are visualized in Amplitude Change Images (ACIs) as illustrated in Figure [<span class="underline">9.5</span>](#figure-9.5-an-illustration-of-amplitude-change-image-analysis). ACIs use grayscale or complementary colors—typically red for features lost between acquisitions and cyan for new features (“red is fled, blue is new”). ACD enables rapid change identification across broad areas and works with modest geometric differences.

<span class="underline">Multi-Temporal SAR (MT-SAR)</span> extends ACD to three or more images to enable time-series analysis. Backscatter profiles reveal temporal patterns such as crop cycles or flood progression. Trend and slope analysis estimate gradual changes in vegetation or infrastructure, while Change Vector Analysis captures magnitude and direction of multi-feature change. Temporal outlier detection flags unusual events, and phenological metrics describe seasonal behavior for agricultural and climate applications.

<span class="underline">Non-Interferometric Displacement Tracking</span> estimates surface motion by correlating amplitude patterns across image pairs, complementing phase-based methods in areas with low coherence or large deformation. Offset tracking uses intensity cross-correlation for large motions such as glacier flow or landslides; speckle tracking leverages speckle consistency for sub-pixel accuracy; and optical-flow approaches compute dense motion fields with variational or machine-learning methods, useful where deformation is complex or decorrelated.

These techniques support a wide range of applications, including flood and water-body mapping, glacier and ice monitoring, landslide and rupture mapping, climate and vegetation trend analysis, agricultural and deforestation monitoring, built-area and infrastructure assessment, land-cover mapping, maritime surveillance, and military reconnaissance.

## 9.7) Phase-Based Interferometric Techniques

<span class="underline">Interferometric SAR (InSAR)</span> techniques use phase differences between two or more coherently acquired images to measure terrain elevation and surface deformation with high precision. They rely on phase-preserving products (See SLC and SICD in Sections [<span class="underline">10.2</span>](10_data_products.md#102-single-look-complex-slc-images) and [<span class="underline">10.3</span>](#sensor-independent-complex-and-derived-data-sicd-sidd)) and processing steps such as co-registration, interferogram formation, phase unwrapping, and noise filtering. When properly applied, InSAR supports high-accuracy topographic mapping and deformation monitoring in diverse environments.

InSAR forms <span class="underline">interferograms</span> from images acquired from slightly different positions, encoding sub-wavelength path length differences. Separating terrain-induced phase from geometric components enables the generation of Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), as demonstrated by missions like SRTM and TanDEM-X. <span class="underline">Coherence maps</span> accompany interferograms to show phase quality, highlight disturbance or vegetation change, and guide suitable image-pair selection. InSAR is widely used for topographic mapping and for detecting elevation changes linked to volcanic activity, tectonics, or glacier flow.

<span class="underline">Differential InSAR (DInSAR)</span> removes topographic phase using an external or previously generated DEM, isolating centimeter- to millimeter-scale ground motion. Typical applications include subsidence from groundwater extraction or tunneling, infrastructure stability assessment, and characterization of seismic or landslide deformation before, during, and after events.

<span class="underline">Persistent Scatterer Interferometry (PSI)</span> analyzes long time series of stable radar reflectors—such as buildings, bridges, or utility infrastructure—to detect displacement rates on the order of millimeters per year. PSI excels at long-term deformation tracking, distinguishing seasonal variation from cumulative trends, generating dense urban deformation maps, and identifying accelerated motion that may signal structural failure.

<span class="underline">Small Baseline Subset (SBAS)</span> techniques form interferograms using image pairs with minimal temporal and spatial separation to reduce decorrelation in vegetated or dynamic areas. Unlike PSI, which focuses on point scatterers, SBAS recovers displacement across distributed scatterers over broad regions. It is used for agricultural and natural-area monitoring, volcanic inflation and deflation mapping, and landslide dynamics where coherent signals persist over slopes.

<span class="underline">Tomographic SAR (TomoSAR)</span> uses multiple baselines with wide angular diversity to reconstruct vertical scattering distributions within resolution cells, producing 3D imaging. TomoSAR enables forest structure analysis for biomass estimation, urban mapping that separates facades and rooftops, and improved topographic modeling in mountainous terrain with complex vertical geometry.

These interferometric methods support applications such as DEM generation, volcanic and tectonic monitoring, glacier dynamics, subsidence and infrastructure stability assessment, seismic and landslide analysis, long-term deformation studies, biomass estimation, and 3D urban modeling.

## 9.8) Other Phase-Based Techniques

Other phase-based SAR methods extend beyond classical interferometry to extract motion, structure, or volumetric properties using specialized acquisition geometries or auxiliary data:

<span class="underline">Polarimetric Interferometry (PolInSAR)</span> merges polarimetric diversity with interferometric phase to characterize vertical scattering in distributed media such as vegetation or snow. By combining multiple polarizations and baselines, PolInSAR retrieves vertical scattering profiles and penetration depths. Applications include forest canopy height and biomass estimation, crop monitoring, and snow or wetland characterization.

<span class="underline">Along-Track Interferometry (ATI)</span> uses two antennas aligned along the flight path to measure Doppler-induced phase shifts, enabling line-of-sight velocity retrieval. ATI supports ocean surface current mapping, sea-ice drift monitoring, and wind-field estimation over water, aiding navigation, climate studies, and meteorological analysis.

<span class="underline">Coherent Change Detection (CCD)</span> identifies subtle scene changes by measuring coherence between co-registered SAR images. Unlike Amplitude Change Detection (ACD), CCD reveals disturbances that alter speckle or small-scale structure without changing brightness. Coherent Change Images (CCIs) depict consistent-phase areas as bright and decorrelated areas as dark, highlighting disruptions such as surface tampering, structural damage, or vegetation disturbance. CCD is sensitive to subtle changes but requires nearly identical geometry and short temporal baselines, making it complementary to interferometric approaches.

These techniques support forest and biomass analysis, agricultural monitoring, ocean and sea-ice dynamics, wind-field mapping, surveillance and security, disaster response, and detection of fine-scale environmental changes.

## 9.9) Signal-Domain Analysis Techniques

<span class="underline">Signal-domain techniques</span> operate directly on the radar phase history or Doppler spectrum rather than on focused images. They exploit temporal, spectral, or aperture-domain variations preserved in the CPHD data product, described in Section [<span class="underline">10.1</span>](10_data_products.md#101-compensated-phase-history-data-cphd), to reveal dynamic scene information that conventional products cannot capture:

<span class="underline">Colorized Sub-Aperture Image Stacks</span> visualize the scene using multiple azimuth sub-aperture images, each highlighting scattering from different viewing angles or Doppler bands. After forming and focusing the sub-aperture stack, selected images are mapped to RGB channels, revealing structure, anisotropy, and motion effects not visible in single-aperture imagery. See Section [<span class="underline">10.5</span>](10_data_products.md#105-colorized-sub-aperture-stack-images-csi) for a level 2 data product based on this technique.

<span class="underline">SAR Video</span> sequences are created by sliding a narrow synthetic aperture window through the CPHD stream to form a rapid sequence of sub-aperture images. The resulting video captures dynamic scene evolution—displacements, rotations, wake patterns, micro-motion, or fast-moving targets—offering a time-resolved view unavailable from standard SAR products. See Section [<span class="underline">10.6</span>](10_data_products.md#106-sar-video-vid) for a productization of this CPHD-based derivative.

<span class="underline">Vibration / Micro-Doppler Signatures</span> characterize small or periodic motions by analyzing fine Doppler variations in the radar return. Vibrating structures, rotating machinery, engine components, and wind-induced motions imprint distinctive time–frequency signatures. Micro-Doppler processing exposes these patterns for structural-health monitoring, target characterization, and other applications requiring dynamic behavior analysis.

<span class="underline">Dynamic Target Motion Extraction</span> estimates the motion, velocity, or trajectory of objects directly from evolving phase-history cues such as Doppler centroid shifts, phase ramps, or sub-aperture displacements. These methods recover paths or instantaneous velocities even when moving objects are blurred or displaced in focused imagery. They are especially useful for analyzing vehicle, vessel, or fast-moving-target dynamics and typically rely on CPHD or sub-aperture stacks.

Together, these signal-domain techniques leverage the full informational content of radar echo data, enabling advanced analysis of motion, dynamics, and direction-dependent scattering beyond what can be derived from conventional image products.
